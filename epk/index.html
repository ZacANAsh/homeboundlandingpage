The Premise

A queer son and the father who couldn't love him out loud.

Ten years after walking out of his own life, Jack — a runaway musician in Thailand — is drawn home by his father's failing health. He means to stay a few days. Then the 2020 lockdown bolts the door behind him, sealing the two most uncommunicative men alive into one house for the first time in a decade. Across a year of silence they edge back toward each other — until a flight out finally opens, and Jack must choose whether to take it. Some things, it turns out, you can't fix with tools.

Score

A whole world inside one room.

Homebound is built for maximum impact and minimal footprint. Four actors, four musicians, and a single unit set — a Tennessee farmhouse that never changes while everything inside it does. The folk / rock score is carried by a live four-piece band: guitar, bass, drums, and cello, playing alongside the cast.

The show is scaled for intimacy. A single room holds an entire decade of distance between a father and son, and the staging asks for nothing more elaborate than that — well suited to a black box, a 199-seat house, or a commercial stage alike.

02 / Synopsis

The Story

Where silence finally meets its deadline.

Are a father and his son too broken to begin again? Logline

Homebound is a chamber musical about a queer son and the father who couldn't love him out loud. It begins in the first weeks of the 2020 lockdown and unfolds across the year that follows.

The show opens with Jack, Frank, Lola, and Jackie standing in isolated pools of light — a musician who stopped playing, a mechanic who only speaks in tools, a wife who disappeared into someone else, a nurse who says she's fine. All four of them are hiding in plain sight (HIDING IN PLAIN SIGHT).

In Koh Samui, Thailand, in February 2020, Jack is fixing boat engines on the beach when his mother Lola calls: Frank is in the ICU. Jack actively refuses to come home — until Lola pushes hard enough that he has no defense left. He picks up his guitar — the first time he's touched it in a long time — and reckons with the version of himself he's been running from for ten years (ALWAYS AN OUTSIDER). He flies home to Tennessee to find his father very much alive in his recliner, watching cable news. Their first encounter is hostile and immediate; ten years of silence breaks open inside three minutes. When Jackie — Jack's childhood best friend, now a frontline nurse — drops in to check Frank's blood pressure, the room briefly opens. After she leaves, Frank tries to say something real for the first time and can't find the words. Jack lets him fail. Jack goes to his old bedroom and calls Lola from Mexico, where she has reinvented herself as a glamorous mezcal-sipping vacationer named “Lola 2.0” (LOLA 2.0). Jack tells her he's not going to fight with his father this time. Some of us have grown. Lola lets the line hang and pivots to her cocktail. Before Jack can plan his exit, the world shuts down (SHELTER IN PLACE). His return flight bounces. He is trapped in his childhood house with the father he hasn't spoken to in a decade.

Jack and Frank fall into an uneasy domestic rhythm — disinfectant wipes, pill schedules, cable news at hostile volume — punctuated by Jack's chosen composure cracking just enough to expose what's underneath (QUARANTINE RULES). A disastrous haircut with poultry shears yields Frank's most honest admission in years. On the porch, Jack picks up the guitar again and finds the first fragment of a song that's been forming in his head (BLUE TRUCK snippet). Jackie reveals that Frank attended her queer wedding — the same man who walked out of Jack's graduation. When Rachel calls, Jackie tries to hold it together — navigating Frank's secret, a manager who calls her wife her “roommate,” and a job that never stops asking — before heading back to the hospital, where she rebuilds the wall in song (FRONT LINE). Meanwhile Lola, alone in her hotel room, briefly cracks when she can't find her lipstick and ends up sitting on the floor (LOLA 2.0 REPRISE).

Jackie arrives at Frank's house carrying a DNR she's signed that morning. She listens to his lungs for the first time — the cover story dropped — and tries one last fight: come to the hospital, see Dr. Levi, drain the fluid. Frank refuses. The hospital pulls Jackie onto longer shifts, and the woman who never breaks finally does. Frank — who has spent the show resisting every gesture of care his son has tried to give him — pumps a dab of sanitizer into his palm and offers it to her in silence. He asks after Rachel. He arranges a dinner he won't be alive to see. Jack walks in with cardboard boxes, oblivious. Sorting through one of them, he finds a camcorder tape with his own name on it. On it, a younger Frank fumbles through a fifth-birthday message and tells a story about his own father and a broken-down truck — about things that look fine on the surface and fall apart inside, and about the work of rebuilding them (NOTHING IS TOO BROKEN). Jack realizes his father has been speaking to him in the only language he knows for his entire life.

At the hospital, Jackie makes a call to a patient's family — explaining what a ventilator does, absorbing a stranger's anger, holding steady for one more person who needs her. When she hangs up, a phone buzzes. She watches it ring until it stops. In Mexico, Lola listens to a voicemail Frank has left her — a message about a photo book she left behind twenty years ago. She listens to it twice, and then, for the first time, she calls him back. Frank picks up. “It's me.” “Laura.” Lights out.

The next morning, Frank produces the DNR from his wallet — it's time — and asks Jack to sign. Jack reveals that he has known about the cancer for months. He googled the names on the pill bottles and chose to wait, because when somebody isn't ready to say a thing, you don't say it for them. The ICU emergency was a lie — scripted by Frank, performed by Lola, to get Jack home before it was too late. Jack confronts Frank not just about the deception but about the deeper wound: the day Frank drove him three towns over to a pastor to be fixed. Frank's answer is not a defense. He didn't have the schematics for a son like Jack, and he didn't understand the damage until Jack was already gone. He apologizes — specifically, naming the act. Then, in the smallest gesture available to him, he asks Jack if he's seeing anyone. “I'd have wanted to meet him.” Jack signs the DNR — not as forgiveness, but as presence.

The weeks that follow are quiet and hard. The three of them exist in separate pools of light as Frank's time runs short (LITTLE BY LITTLE). In his final moments, Frank asks Jack to read aloud a yellowed scrap of paper he has carried in his wallet for twenty years — a poem Jack wrote at age seven about a blue truck and the moon. Jack reads it. Frank laughs once and lets go.

The morning of the funeral, Lola arrives in full Mexico Glamour and quickly unravels. She describes standing in Row F at graduation, watching Jack perform, knowing exactly what Frank's silence meant — and picking up her purse anyway. Not because she agreed, but because she didn't yet know how to be a person who disagreed. Jack forgives her — the first act of grace he's learned from watching his father try to reach him in the only language he knew (FROM HERE). At the funeral, Jack forgives Frank publicly, and announces he is staying.

In the days that follow, Jack picks up the phone and books a show at the Rusty Nail — the same stage where he last performed, the same town that watched him leave. Lola calls Miguel to say she's staying a little longer. At the Rusty Nail, Jackie steps to the microphone and tells the room what she has never said out loud: that watching Jack perform in eyeliner at graduation, shaking but not stopping, was the night she stopped pretending to be the daughter her parents said they wanted. She finds Rachel in the crowd, calls her my wife, and points — claiming publicly what she'd spent years holding quietly. Jack takes the stage, names his mother in the audience — here tonight, even though his father isn't anymore — and transforms his father's legacy and a seven-year-old's terrible poem into a roaring rock anthem about rust, roots, and choosing to stop running (BLUE TRUCK).

04 / Creative Team

The Makers

Who's behind the show.

Zach Adam

Book · Music · Lyrics

Site Instagram

Zach Adam is a Billboard Songwriting Contest winner, multi-Top-20 hitmaker, PMG recording artist and BMG staff writer, and recipient of ACUM Composer of the Year and Song of the Year awards.

As a writer-composer of musical theater, he wrote book, music, and lyrics for Homebound, a chamber musical about a queer son and his estranged father trapped together by the 2020 lockdown, selected from hundreds of submissions for the 2026 Broadway Bound Theatre Festival at AMT Theater in NYC. He is co-writer and composer of Voice!, chosen as the sole musical selection for AMDA SummerFest 2026. Founder, The New Theater Nashville.

Shea Sullivan

Director

Site

Shea Sullivan is a NYC-based director and choreographer. Shea is no stranger to developing new work and is passionate about bringing new pieces to life, including two recent world premieres: Last of the Red Hot Mamas at Bucks County Playhouse and YES! The Musical in Nashville, TN. She is also currently developing a new 1960s-inspired piece entitled New World Comin'.

Her work has been seen Off-Broadway and throughout the NYC theater scene, including R.R.R.E.D., Neurosis (Chita Rivera Award nomination), Polkadots: The Cool Kids Musical (Off Broadway Alliance Award), Pageant! (SDC Callaway Finalist; Drama Desk nomination for Best Revival), I'll Say She Is, Once Upon a Mattress, and Pete(her) Pan.

Regional credits include Paper Mill Playhouse, Ford's Theatre, Westchester Broadway Theatre, Cape Playhouse, Maltz Jupiter Theatre, Bucks County Playhouse, Barrington Stage Company, Lyric Theatre, Arts Center of Coastal Carolina, and Bay Street Theater, among others. Upcoming projects include Rock of Ages at The REV Theatre Company, as well as Rock of Ages and Holiday Inn at Bucks County Playhouse.

Shea resides in NYC with her partner Katie and their pup, Peanut.
